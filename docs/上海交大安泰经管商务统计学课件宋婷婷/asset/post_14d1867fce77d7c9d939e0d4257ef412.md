## When the Parametric t-test Goes Wrong

- Test statistics: t-statistic

$$
t = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{S^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \quad \text{where} \quad S^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}
$$

- In the dataset A: $s_1^2 = 9.2$ and $s_2^2 = 17.5$
- In the dataset B: $s_1^2 = 9.2$ and $s_2^2 = 2335$
- **Conclusion:** the mean test statistics are highly influenced by the extreme outliers.

## When to Use Nonparametric Methods

Principles: if you can apply both parametric and nonparametric

- With **correct assumptions** (e.g., normal distribution), parametric methods will be more **efficient** and **powerful** than nonparametric method.
- If the normality assumption is grossly violated (e.g., extreme outliers), nonparametric tests can be much more **efficient** and **powerful** than the corresponding parametric test.

If you ONLY have qualitative data, the nonparametric test may be the only choice.

## Sign Test: Testing for a Population Median

- The Lawler Grocery Store chain made the decision to carry a new potato chip product, based on the manufacturer’s estimate that the median sales should be $450 per week on a per store basis.
- Three months later, Lawler’s management requested the hypothesis test for the population median weekly sales:

$H_0: \text{median} = 450, \quad \text{versus} \quad H_a: \text{median} \neq 450$

- Data: randomly selected one-week sales from $n = 10$ Lawler’s stores

| Store Number | Weekly Sales ($) | Store Number | Weekly Sales ($) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 56 | 485 | 63 | 474 |
| 19 | 562 | 39 | 662 |
| 36 | 415 | 84 | 380 |
| 128 | 880 | 102 | 515 |
| 12 | 426 | 44 | 721 |

## Test with Binomial Distribution

- For each observation, record a sign “+” if it is greater than 450, and record a sign “−” otherwise.
- If the median is 450, there should be 50% plus signs and 50% minus signs.
- Generally, the number of “+”, $X$, follows $B(n,p)$ for some $p$.
- The sign test converts the original hypotheses about the median into the hypotheses about the binomial probability $p$:

$H_0: p = 0.5$, versus $H_a: p \ne 0.5$
