
## Price Inflation

- We lose nothing by setting p₁=1 so that p₂ = 1+ π.
- Then we can rewrite the budget constraint
  p₁c₁ + (p₂ / 1+r) c₂ = m₁ + (m₂ / 1+r)
  as
  c₁ + ((1+π) / 1+r) c₂ = m₁ + (m₂ / 1+r)

c₁ + (1 + π)/(1 + r) c₂ = m₁ + m₂/(1 + r)

rearranges to

c₂ = - (1 + r)/(1 + π) c₁ + [(1 + r)m₁ + m₂]/(1 + π)

so the slope of the intertemporal budget constraint is

- (1 + r)/(1 + π)

- When there was no price inflation (p₁=p₂=1) the slope of the budget constraint was -(1+r).
- Now, with price inflation, the slope of the budget constraint is -(1+r)/(1+ π). This can be written as

−(1 + ρ) = − (1 + r) / (1 + π)

ρ is known as the real interest rate.

## Real Interest Rate

```
-(1 + ρ) = - (1 + r)/(1 + π)
```

gives

```
ρ = (r - π)/(1 + π).
```

For low inflation rates (π ≈ 0), ρ ≈ r - π. For higher inflation rates this approximation becomes poor.

## Valuing Securities

- The security is equivalent to the sum of three securities;
  - the first pays only $m₁ at the end of year 1,
  - the second pays only $m₂ at the end of year 2, and
  - the third pays only $m₃ at the end of year 3.

- The PV of $m₁ paid 1 year from now is m₁ / (1+r)
- The PV of $m₂ paid 2 years from now is m₂ / (1+r)²
- The PV of $m₃ paid 3 years from now is m₃ / (1+r)³
- The PV of the security is therefore m₁ / (1+r) + m₂ / (1+r)² + m₃ / (1+r)³.

## Valuing Bonds

- A bond is a special type of security that pays a fixed amount $x for T years (its maturity date) and then pays its face value $F.
- What is the most that should now be paid for such a bond?

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_589_0.png)

| End of Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | … | T-1 | T |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Income Paid | $x | $x | $x | $x | $x | $F |
| Present-Value | $\frac{x}{1+r}$ | $\frac{x}{(1+r)^2}$ | $\frac{x}{(1+r)^3}$ | … | $\frac{x}{(1+r)^{T-1}}$ | $\frac{F}{(1+r)^T}$ |

$$PV = \frac{x}{1+r} + \frac{x}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{x}{(1+r)^3} + \cdots + \frac{x}{(1+r)^{T-1}} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^T}.$$
