
## An Example

- Two consumers
  - Person 1 has low willingness-to-pay
  - Person 2 has high willingness-to-pay
- Assume MC = 0

## Second-degree Price Discrimination

- Charge A for x₁⁰ hoping to get person 1.
- Charge A + B + C for x₂⁰ hoping to get person 2.
- But person 2 is better off buying x₁⁰ and receiving a CS = B.
- Fails to let consumers self-select themselves.
- Can alternatively charge A + C for x₂⁰ to identify person 2.
- Profit = 2A
- Profit = 2A + C

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1492_0.png)

- Reduce x₁⁰ so A is reduced by a little but C can be increased by a lot. Persons 1 and 2 are still identified.
- Profit is higher.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1493_0.png)

- Profit is maximized at x₁ᵐ.
- $/output unit, MC(y), quantity, A, B, C, x₁ᵐ, x₂⁰

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1494_0.png)

- Second-degree price discrimination can also occur in the dimension of quality.
- Different prices for different seats in a theater.
- Different prices for economy class vs. business class seats in airplanes.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1495_0.png)

## Third-degree Price Discrimination

- Price paid by buyers in a given group is the same for all units purchased. But price may differ across buyer groups.
- Quality of goods is the same across groups.
- Can identify groups but no further identification within that group.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1496_0.png)

- A monopolist manipulates market price by altering the quantity of product supplied to that market.
- So the question “What discriminatory prices will the monopolist set, one for each group?” is really the question “How many units of product will the monopolist supply to each group?”

- Two markets, 1 and 2.
- y₁ is the quantity supplied to market 1. Market 1’s inverse demand function is p₁(y₁).
- y₂ is the quantity supplied to market 2. Market 2’s inverse demand function is p₂(y₂).

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1498_0.png)

- For given supply levels y₁ and y₂, the firm’s profit is:

  Π(y₁, y₂) = p₁(y₁)y₁ + p₂(y₂)y₂ − c(y₁ + y₂).

- What values of y₁ and y₂ maximize profit?

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1499_0.png)

### The profit-maximization conditions are

Π(y₁, y₂) = p₁(y₁)y₁ + p₂(y₂)y₂ − c(y₁ + y₂).

∂Π/∂y₁ = ∂/∂y₁ (p₁(y₁)y₁) − [∂c(y₁ + y₂)/∂(y₁ + y₂)] × [∂(y₁ + y₂)/∂y₁] = 0

∂Π/∂y₂ = ∂/∂y₂ (p₂(y₂)y₂) − [∂c(y₁ + y₂)/∂(y₁ + y₂)] × [∂(y₁ + y₂)/∂y₂] = 0

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1500_0.png)

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1501_0.png)

Since ∂(y₁ + y₂)/∂y₁ = 1 and ∂(y₁ + y₂)/∂y₂ = 1, the profit-maximization conditions become:

∂/∂y₁ (p₁(y₁)y₁) = ∂c(y₁ + y₂)/∂(y₁ + y₂)

and ∂/∂y₂ (p₂(y₂)y₂) = ∂c(y₁ + y₂)/∂(y₁ + y₂).

∂/∂y₁ (p₁(y₁)y₁) = ∂/∂y₂ (p₂(y₂)y₂) = ∂c(y₁ + y₂)/∂(y₁ + y₂)

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1503_0.png)

MR₁(y₁) = MR₂(y₂) says that the allocation y₁, y₂ maximizes the revenue from selling y₁ + y₂ output units.

E.g., if MR₁(y₁) > MR₂(y₂), then an output unit should be moved from market 2 to market 1 to increase total revenue.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1504_0.png)
