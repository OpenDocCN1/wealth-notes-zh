## Walras’ Law

- Walras’ Law is an identity; i.e. a statement that is true for any positive prices (p₁,p₂), whether these are equilibrium prices or not.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1884_0.png)

## Walras’ Law

p₁x₁*ᴬ + p₂x₂*ᴬ = p₁ω₁ᴬ + p₂ω₂ᴬ
p₁x₁*ᴮ + p₂x₂*ᴮ = p₁ω₁ᴮ + p₂ω₂ᴮ

## Summing gives

p₁(x₁*ᴬ + x₁*ᴮ) + p₂(x₂*ᴬ + x₂*ᴮ)
= p₁(ω₁ᴬ + ω₁ᴮ) + p₂(ω₂ᴮ + ω₂ᴮ).

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1886_0.png)

## Walras’ Law

p₁(x₁*ᴬ + x₁*ᴮ) + p₂(x₂*ᴬ + x₂*ᴮ)
= p₁(ω₁ᴬ + ω₁ᴮ) + p₂(ω₂ᴮ + ω₂ᴮ).

Rearranged,

p₁(x₁*ᴬ + x₁*ᴮ − ω₁ᴬ − ω₁ᴮ) +
p₂(x₂*ᴬ + x₂*ᴮ − ω₂ᴬ − ω₂ᴮ) = 0.

## Walras’ Law

$$p_1(x_1^{*A}+x_1^{*B}-\omega_1^A-\omega_1^B)+p_2(x_2^{*A}+x_2^{*B}-\omega_2^A-\omega_2^B)=0.$$

This says that the summed market value of excess demands is zero for **any** positive prices p₁ and p₂ -- this is Walras’ Law.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1888_0.png)

## Implications of Walras’ Law

So one implication of Walras’ Law for a two-commodity exchange economy is that if one market is in equilibrium then the other market must also be in equilibrium.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1890_0.png)

## Implications of Walras’ Law

What if, for some positive prices p₁ and p₂, there is an excess quantity supplied of commodity 1? That is,
 x₁*ᴬ + x₁*ᴮ − ω₁ᴬ − ω₁ᴮ < 0.

Then
 p₁(x₁*ᴬ + x₁*ᴮ − ω₁ᴬ − ω₁ᴮ) + 
 p₂(x₂*ᴬ + x₂*ᴮ − ω₂ᴬ − ω₂ᴮ) = 0

implies
 x₂*ᴬ + x₂*ᴮ − ω₂ᴬ − ω₂ᴮ > 0.

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1891_0.png)

## Implications of Walras’ Law

So a second implication of Walras’ Law for a two-commodity exchange economy is that an excess supply in one market implies an excess demand in the other market.

## Misperception of Random Sequences

## Intuitive beliefs about randomness not accurate

- E.g. flip a (fair) coin and get 5 heads in a row
- tails is more likely (gambler’s fallacy)
- heads is more likely (reverse gambler’s fallacy, hot outcome)
- disposition effect in finance (Odean 1998, Shefrin and Statman 1985)

- E.g. betting on flips of a (fair) coin and win 5 times in a row
- a win is more likely (hot hand)
- a loss is more likely (stock of luck)
- choice of mutual fund managers (Sirri and Tufano 1998) (Cahart 1997)

## Two different biases

- Gambler’s fallacy (hot outcome)
- Hot hand (stock of luck)

## Empirical data from casino gambling

## The Data

Videotapes (security camera) of American roulette from large casino in Reno, Nevada (7/98)

- 18 hours of roulette play (iid)
- 139 gamblers
- 904 spins of wheel (approx one per minute)
- 24,131 bets

## Could observe

- Buying in and out
- Color and location of chips placed on layout
- Winning number

## Could not observe

- Faces of individuals

## Roulette Apparatus

Wheel with 38 sections (1-36, 0, 00) + white ball

![](img/e3f3f402f74cf453caf4596d2d7d7398_1898_0.png)

Layout on which bets are placed

## Roulette Logistics

Gamblers offer dealer money, get colored chips (unique), place chips on layout (min, max bets)

Dealer spins wheel and rolls ball around edge, ball falls into one numbered slot

Dealer pays gamblers who have bet on that number, takes rest of bets, begins again

Gamblers can leave at any time by exchanging their roulette chips for casino chips
