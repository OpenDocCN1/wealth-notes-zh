# They took the odor out of ammonia

and created a new, pine-fragrant formula that is changing the housecleaning habits of American homemakers

![](bbox=[0.42, 0.35, 0.48, 0.45])

FOR GENERATIONS, no American homemaker considered her home really clean without the aid of household ammonia. Any grandmother will recall that in her day it was as necessary to household cleanliness as soap to personal cleanliness.

In the business boom of the fifties, a new crop of all-purpose household cleaners, many much higher priced, began to displace ammonia on the housewife's cleaning shelf. Why?

The reason was obvious. Ammonia had one objectionable feature: its strong, pungent odor made it unpleasant to use.

In Scranton, Pennsylvania, the problem threatened the livelihood of two ex-GI's, Alvin and Leonard Stahler, who had invested all their savings after the war in a chemical plant whose major product was household ammonia.

They asked themselves. "Could this odor be changed and a more pleasant odor substituted?"

The problem was not an easy one. The new fragrance had to integrate perfectly with the atomic structure of ammonia. It had to have a "clean smell," light and fresh, with no cosmetic heaviness.

The brothers went to work with perfume houses both here and abroad, and, after testing almost one hundred fragrances, discovered an essence from Switzerland's famed pine trees which ideally fulfilled both requirements.
