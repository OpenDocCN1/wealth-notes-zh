# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The digital revolution is changing every aspect of our lives. International trade, as one of the main pillars of a globalized world, is at the center of this revolution, as cross-border transactions of goods and services become an integral, often invisible, part of our daily digital routine.

Digital trade benefits from a comprehensive, modern and non-discriminatory regulatory framework. Regulation can provide the legal tools necessary for remote contracts, clarify the rights and obligations of the multiple actors involved in digital transactions, and establish a framework that promotes trust in digital markets, even when the consumer does not know the merchant or that the merchant is in a different country. However, regulation can also further strengthen segmentation of digital trade, de facto restricting digital transactions.

The report presents the Digital Trade Regulatory Readiness (DTRR) database compiled by a team of World Bank experts. The database offers novel information on laws and regulations on digital trade for 121 economies around the world, out of which 95 are developing countries. The database reflects countries' regulatory framework on specific matters directly relevant to digital trade, structure on nine "regulatory areas" (i.e., i) electronic documents; ii) electronic signature & digital ID; iii) paperless trade; iv) online consumer protection; v) data protection; vi) artificial Intelligence; vii) cybersecurity & cybercrime; viii) cross-border data; and ix) intermediary liability) distributed along three policy pillars, namely electronic transactions, trust-building regulation, and platform regulation. Altogether, the DTRR offers more than 13,000 datapoints, including over 5,200 regulatory **ES**.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

**The digital revolution is changing every aspect of our lives.** The COVID pandemic and its stay-at-home measures demonstrated how communications technologies can help shrink distances. International trade, as one of the main pillars of a globalized world, is at the center of this revolution, as cross-border transactions of goods and services become an integral, often invisible, part of our daily digital routine.

**In a rapidly changing digital world, governments confront new challenges in the regulation of digital trade.** Digital devices come alive with services from around the world—with software covering the whole spectrum from entertainment to productivity. Classic trade problems like expediting trade procedures take on new resonance as shipments cross international borders in varied forms—individually packaged to consumers rather than in palleted cargo to wholesalers. Obstacles to trade are taking new shapes: issues like cross-border data flows and cybersecurity require international coordination to ensure the efficient and timely flow of information. Furthermore, the policy challenges lie not only in fostering cross-border transactions, but also in ensuring that benefits and opportunities from digital trade are equitably distributed in society.

### A. Digital trade and regulation

**Multiple regulatory areas are crucial to the development of digital trade.** Digital trade, understood as the purchase and/or delivery of goods and services through digital means (see Box 1 for a definition), benefits from a comprehensive, modern and non-discriminatory regulatory framework. Regulation can provide the legal tools necessary for remote contracts, clarify the rights and obligations of the multiple actors involved in digital transactions, and establish a framework that promotes trust in digital markets,¹ even when the consumer does not know the merchant or that the merchant is in a different country. However, regulation can also reinforce the fragmentation of digital trade, effectively restricting digital transactions.

**Rules affecting digital trade extends beyond measures explicitly targeting foreign transactions.** As is often the case in services trade more broadly, digital trade is typically governed by general laws and regulations that shape market conditions for a given activity—regardless of whether that activity is carried out by domestic or foreign providers. In this context, what makes a regulation relevant to digital trade is the inherently cross-border nature of the activities it covers. For example, legal liability rules for intermediary platforms often apply to domestic and foreign services alike. However, due to the borderless nature of digital platforms, such rules are more likely to affect foreign transactions than purely domestic ones.

**Regulation plays three essential roles in digital transactions**:

- **Providing essential regulatory tools for remote digital transactions.** One of the roles of economic regulation is providing legal recognition of innovative ways of making business interactions—while ensuring that the new practices are not harmful to third parties or can be otherwise deemed contrary to public interest. In the digital era, tools like *electronic documents* and *electronic signatures* require a regulation that affords them legal recognition to do business. Lack of electronic signatures may mean, for instance, that parties may need to physically travel or mail a hard copy to sign a document, increasing time and costs for the transaction. Further, the ability to complete *paperless trade* procedures is also a supporting element for cross-border B2B trade.

- **Enhancing trust in digital markets by ensuring that consumers are protected and that their information is safe and remains private.** Lack of trust in digital markets remains the top reason for not engaging in online purchases. In a 24-country survey, almost half the respondents said they never buy goods or services online because they do not trust online transactions (CIGI-Ipsos, 2017). **An enabling environment for digital trade requires coordination with multiple other regulatory policies.** Regulation relevant to digital trade extends well beyond the current scope of the DTRR. For example, an effective regulatory and institutional framework for electronic payments is essential to cross-border transactions.

There are four forms of digital trade: the Internet enables trade in "traditional" goods and services that are digitally ordered but delivered offline, and it also provides a platform for trading purely "digitally traded" goods and services that are not only ordered electronically but also produced and delivered online.

Under most definitions of e-commerce, electronic transactions can entail the sale of either goods or services. For traditional goods and services, the Internet functions as a global e-commerce marketplace that enables remote transactions of goods and services that would otherwise be sold in brick-and-mortar stores. Digital technologies not only expand the possibilities for trade in existing goods and services but also offer a completely new environment for new "digital" goods and services to be developed—and traded.

Figure B1 illustrates trade in digital goods and services in quadrants III and IV, respectively. Business reality, especially in the digital environment, however, often blurs these conceptual lines. Software, for example, has been historically traded as merchandise (embodied in the shipping of physical media such as floppy disks or CDs) but is now often provided as a service, based on a subscription fee, that includes the right to updates, technical support, and ancillary digital products. Similarly, some services such as ICT consumer support can blur the lines between "traditional" and "digitally traded" services, as reflected in quadrants I and IV. Finally, some business can be used by final consumers or by other businesses, making it a challenge to clearly distinguish between B2C and B2B activities. This approach is consistent with a similar classification offered by the OECD, WTO, and IMF that focuses on whether goods and services are digitally ordered and/or digitally delivered (Figure B2).

Identifying the differences among the various types of digital trade is more than theoretically valuable. Rather, realizing these different phenomena as part of the broader digital trade or even digital economy analysis has practical implications. For instance, for statistical purposes, e-commerce goods transactions are normally captured by customs as they cross the border in the form of parcels and fall under the goods sections of the current account. Digital goods such as software or e-books should also, in principle, be captured as goods under the current account; however, because they are digitally delivered and do not cross physical borders, customs information will not be available, and such information should be sought elsewhere—for instance, in the international payments for goods and services as recorded by central banks. More importantly, the different expressions of digital trade also face different challenges and call for different policy responses: the logistical challenges of international parcel shipping faced by online retailers are not necessarily relevant to traders of digital goods and services, who, instead, may be much more concerned with intellectual property rights and contract enforcement.

# 2. A comprehensive and modern digital regulatory framework is essential for supporting digital trade.

Regulation plays three critical roles for digital transactions. It provides the legal tools needed for concluding valid and enforceable electronic contracts, such as rules giving effect to the electronic authentication of documents and e-signatures. Frameworks for personal data protection, online consumer protection, and cybersecurity offer safeguards that enhance trust in digital markets. Finally, regulations on the legal liability of platforms for unlawful or harmful content and rules on the international transfers of data set out basic rules that support a transparent and predictable framework for digital markets.

**Regulation can also introduce unwarranted limitations to digital trade.** For instance, unnecessarily restrictive or overly cumbersome requirements for cross-border data transfers limit access to certain digitally delivered services. Limitations on the types of goods and services offered online may be warranted by public policy goals (e.g., restrictions on guns or controlled substances) or may add costs and inefficiencies by segmenting markets (e.g., requiring the use of domestic cloud services). The central challenge for policymakers and regulators is therefore to adopt balanced frameworks that minimize restrictions on digital trade while offering a comprehensive framework for digital transactions and sound safeguards for users. By requiring that public policy measures minimize unnecessary trade restrictions, trade agreements help establish useful benchmarks for setting regulatory boundaries.

**The DTRR database focuses on horizontal regulations that provide essential regulatory tools for remote transactions, promote trust in digital markets, and define rules for online platforms.** The data gathered helps inform global progress on digital governance by identifying regulatory regimes that lack elements essential to digital trade, thereby introducing costs in the form of legal uncertainty for consumers and providers. In this context, "readiness" is intended as the ability of the regulation to comprehensively address the challenges presented by digital technologies in line with international best practices.

**The database offers novel information on laws and regulations on digital trade around the world.** The database reflects countries' regulatory framework on specific matters directly relevant to digital trade, specifically in the areas of electronic transactions, trust-building regulation, and platform regulation. The DTRR offers a total of over novel 13,000 datapoints, which includes over 5,000 regulatory measures effectively in place with explicit reference ("traced") to the specific legal instrument, including its section and paragraph, that supports them. The basic premise is that providing effective and transparent regulatory solutions fosters digital trade by enhancing trust in digital markets and reducing costs related to uncertainty and ambiguity in the legal framework.

**The goal of the DTRR is to inform policymaking by identifying priorities for strengthening regulation on digital trade**. Readily assessing progress on digital regulations, while also providing granular information on specific regulatory solutions (or lack therefore) within a country's legal framework, the DTRR can help policymakers, international experts, practitioners and academics to identify regulatory priorities for strengthening regulation on digital trade. The database also provides a starting point for assessing compliance of a country's regulatory framework with international obligations, such as digital trade agreements or the plurilateral WTO e-commerce agreement. In that sense, the DTRR focuses on offering *qualitative* information for over 120 economies in the world in an accessible and user-friendly manner. The data collection is based on laws and regulations related to digital trade, from which the DTRR identifies the specific regulatory measures as set out in formal legal instruments. As such, the information presented in the DTRR reflects *de jure* frameworks only.

**The database captures regulations as they apply to digital trade, even when they may have a broader scope.** In an effort to zero in on digital trade, the regulation solutions covered address matters inherent to digital trade. In some instances, however, matters relative to digital trade are covered under general, non-digital-specific rules. This is particularly the case in consumer protection, where broad, often dated, consumer-protection regimes also apply to digital transactions, even if they were not designed to do so. The DTRR still recognizes the regulation as applying to digital trade and records it positively wherever the regulatory solution is met, even if contained in a general law or regulation that goes beyond digital trade. This methodological approach is understood to reflect more accurately the regulatory framework applying to digital trade. In practice, however, general frameworks generally fall short of good practices in digital regulation.

**The DTRR focuses on qualitative information but is designed to support further use in quantitative analyses.** Unlike other databases in this space—such as the OECD Digital STRI, ECIPE's DTRI, and the WB-WTO STRI—the DTRR does not provide scores or indexes of the regulatory measures it collects. However, the data can be further processed to support econometric and other quantitative studies aimed at assessing the quality of regulatory frameworks. To this end, the full dataset is available for download, and the authors encourage its use and further development.

### i. Regulatory solutions

**The DTRR focuses on the "regulatory solutions" featured in a country's legal framework.** The main information offered by the DTRR is whether a country has adopted specific statutory actions or safeguards ("regulatory solutions") meant to overcome policy challenges related to digital trade. Going beyond the title or general context of the legislation, the DTRR digs into the content of laws and regulation to identify whether such regulatory solutions are in place. Regulatory solutions can take the form of statutory requirements, like the mandate to disclose information certain about the vendor in e-commerce platforms or that electronic documents have the same legal value as physical one, or the adoption of certain safeguards, like granting a right to individuals to request the deletion of their personal information. While the database builds on the assumptions that adopting these regulatory solutions acts as an enabler for digital trade, the section on cross-border data rules also identifies measures that are in fact restrictions on digital trade. These few "negative" regulatory solutions are treated separately from the bulk of the data in most analyses—see section 3.a.iv for details.

**The focus on regulatory solutions allows us to assess how comprehensive and modern the regulation is.** Regulations can range from basic laws that provide solutions to a few aspects of a given policy area, to frameworks that seek to provide a comprehensive response to various new challenges brought about by digital trade. Consumer trust in remote, digital transactions, for example, is lower than in face-to-face interactions, as the consumer has often little information about its counterpart. Explicitly recognizing that online transactions are covered by consumer protection and requiring online vendors to provide certain information are simple regulatory solutions featured in virtually all effective consumer protection framework; more advanced regulations, however, include rules against the use of "dark patterns" (i.e., user interface designs intended to manipulate behavior—for examples making very easy to sign up for a service, but very difficult to cancel it) or rules against spam. By identifying which regulatory solutions are in place, the DTRR allows users to assess how comprehensive and modern the regulation actually is with greater certainty than verifying the mere existence (or lack thereof) of a legal instrument on any given area.

## B. Regulation on electronic transactions

Ensuring that electronic documents and signatures are fully recognized and enforceable—while providing citizens with adequate tools—is therefore essential to underpinning the legal validity and practical use of remote electronic contracts and transactions.

### i. Electronic documents

**A strong and reliable framework for e-documents is particularly important for business-to-business (B2B) transactions.** Transactions with final consumers, such as those on e-commerce platforms like Amazon.com, Lazada.com, or MercadoLibre.com, and app-based services like Airbnb, do not typically entail major documentation exchanges and can be concluded even without a specific regulatory framework for electronic transactions. However, B2B transactions that entail customization of products and services provided over time, such as those that allow suppliers to connect to global value chains or that require peripatetic delivery over extended periods, the ability to conclude or amend contracts across borders in a secure and reliable way is essential for reducing costs and facilitating cross-border business operations.

### ii. Electronic signatures & digital authentication

**The ability to conclude legally binding contracts remotely depends heavily on capturing the parties' agreement in a secure and reliable manner.** Electronic signatures contribute to remote transactions by providing a legal and technological mechanism that ensure the parties that their identities and expressions of will be secure and reliable, even in contracts concluded at a distance or across countries. Digital identities entail a collection of electronically captured and stored attributes that uniquely describe a person within a given context and are used for electronic transactions (World Bank, 2016), which allow for digital IDs that can be used to prove real-life identity online, and thereby grant access to a range of public and private services in a safe and secure way.

**Box 2:** The EU's eIDAS Regulation: A framework for cross border E-ID and Trust Services

The European Union enacted Regulation No. 910/2014 on 'electronic identification and trust services for electronic transactions in the internal market' ("eIDAS") to remove barriers to the cross-border use of electronic identification means for public services used in the EU Member States. The eIDAS sought to meet this goal by prescribing conditions under which Member States must recognize electronic identification schemes of another EU Member State, laying down rules for trust services for electronic transactions, and by establishing a legal framework for, amongst others, electronic signatures, and documents. The eIDAS regulation requires that mandatory electronic identification schemes to be notified to the Commission along with the appropriate assurance level: either low, substantial and/or high. These assurance levels correspond to minimum technical specifications, standards, and procedures notified by the Commission. Each of the notified electronic identification schemes are required to be interoperable, such that there is no discrimination between any specific national technical solutions for electronic identification. In practice, this has enabled citizens with smartcards and mobile-phone-based digital identification systems issued in one EU Member State to access online services provided in other European countries. By virtue of the Agreement on the European Economic Area, the eIDAS has also been adopted as law in Norway, Iceland, and the Principality of Liechtenstein.

The eIDAS regulation may have also helped countries in establishing electronic identification systems through the legal framework of qualified trust service providers. The eIDAS defines a 'trust service' as an electronic service relating to the creation, verification, preservation, and validation of electronic signatures, electronic seals, and certificates for website authentication. Trust service providers that successfully fulfill the accreditation requirements of the conformity assessment body are listed as 'qualified trust service providers' by each EU Member State. The reliability and credibility associated with being a qualified trust service provider under eIDAS has also made it easier for EU Member States to engage qualified trust service providers to establish digital identity solutions. For instance, the Republic of North Macedonia engaged Evrotrust, a qualified trust service provider of the Mastercard group, to launch the country's first remote digital identity services system.

### iii. Paperless trade

**In paperless trade, digital information is used instead of paper documents to fulfill customs procedures.** Digital customs procedures are a valuable complement not only to traditional, containerized, bulk trade that moves thousands of products at once but also to international e-commerce, where goods are often sold and shipped in individual parcels directly to the final consumer. Bottlenecks in customs clearance and regulatory documentation can be particularly challenging for smaller businesses and digital traders with less experience and resources. (UNECE and WEF, 2017).

## C. Trust building regulation

**A robust regulatory framework that prevents the misuse of data while facilitating its use and sharing is essential for building trust in data transactions.** Despite many recent important advances regarding personal data, legal frameworks for data governance across much of the developing world remain inconsistent, raising concerns about the ability of lower-income countries to benefit from the development opportunities emerging from the burgeoning global data economy (World Bank, 2021).

**Regulation plays an essential role in bolstering digital markets by promoting trust.** Because digital markets are still in their infancy, lack of trust in remote electronic transactions remains the primary reason consumers avoid online purchases—especially across borders (Figure 7). The very nature of remote electronic transactions means that consumers have no face-to-face contact with vendors, meaning that there are no "visual cues," such as location, facilities, and personalized interaction, that help consumers gauge a retailer or supplier's professionalism. In this environment, consumers are asked to disclose sensitive information and personal data either to a retailer, online intermediary, or digital platform. As a result, one important limiting factor in both developed and developing economies is the perception that cross-border online transactions and delivery are less secure, and that remedies do not exist when something goes wrong (World Economic Forum, 2019).

**Trust-building regulation for digital markets focuses on three goals: overcoming information asymmetries, strengthening individuals' rights and remedies, and making online transactions secure.** Under this "trust building" category, the database captures policies on consumer protection, data protection, cybersecurity and AI. Overall, by ensuring that such data is adequately protected and may not be used for purposes other than the specific issue at hand, these regulations seek to provide online users a sense of security in the transaction and control over their own information.

### ii. Data protection

**Consumers are increasingly aware of the implications of unrestricted access to personal data.** Lack of trust in the way personal data is managed drives users away from electronic transactions, limiting the growth of digital markets. Safeguards governing when and how firms may collect, process and share personal data are essential for building digital markets in which users feel safe providing their personal information, knowing it will only be used and shared as necessary for the original purpose.

**Strong and consistent data protection regulations are also essential from digital trade, especially for digital services.** Some studies have found that countries that share strong safeguards for personal data tend to trade more between each other (World Bank, 2021; Ferracane and van der Marel, 2021). Two factors may play a role in this phenomenon. On one hand, consumers are more likely to transact with vendors who, whether due to good practices or regulatory compliance, provide clear assurances about how personal data is used and with whom it may be shared. In parallel, as data protection regimes expand globally, a growing number of countries now require that foreign partners offer comparable—or "adequate"—protections before allowing cross-border data transfers. This creates incentives to trade with countries that maintain similar data protection standards.

### iii. Artificial intelligence

**Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to boost efficiency and productivity and opens the door to new tradeable services.** The ability of machines to "learn" by identifying patterns of data—from a human face, to the risk associated with certain activities, to the potential interests of consumers—can deliver significant productivity gains for businesses. For instance, employee recruitment often involves the costly review of dozens of candidate profiles, a process that can be automated using AI solutions. Similarly, machine learning algorithms can parse large amounts of mobile-phone data to deliver instantaneous credit scores to users in developing countries (Strusani and Houngbonon, 2019). Digital trade plays a critical double role in AI. As machine learning feeds from, and is improved by, large amounts of data, access to information around the world is essential to a well-trained system. Furthermore, because a handful of tech giants control big data and advanced algorithms, most AI users around the world are more likely to access AI services through cross-border digital trade than by developing domestic systems, at least in the short to medium term. At the same time, increasing concerns around data privacy, algorithmic bias, and automated decision making have led to growing regulatory scrutiny of AI and data flows. These tensions underscore the importance of developing international frameworks that enable digital trade while addressing legitimate governance concerns.

### iv. Cybersecurity & cybercrime

**While less visible to individual consumers, cybersecurity regulation is essential to promoting trust in digital trade.** Cybersecurity rules include a broad range of measures addressing the security of network infrastructure and the elements over which data flow, as well as policies to secure the storage and the safe transfer of data. From this perspective, cybersecurity policies seek to bolster broad public confidence in the use of the internet and ICT by addressing the risks that crime, and even armed conflicts may bring to the security of networks such as power grids and urban utilities; devices from consumer phones to computer mainframes; and both personal and non-personal data (World Bank and United Nations, 2017). From a digital trade perspective, however, the focus is narrower—individuals' trust and measures to protect personal data from unauthorized access. In that sense, major data breaches, like the one Yahoo! witnessed in 2013 that affected 3 billion user accounts, not only compromise people's privacy but can have a chilling effect on digital markets as consumers realize how vulnerable their information can be.

## D. Platform regulation

**Online platforms play an essential role in digital trade by connecting individual consumers with retailers, manufacturers, and service providers from around the world.** The internet's unparalleled ability to connect billions of individuals worldwide has boosted business models based on intermediation between vendors and consumers. E-commerce platforms like Alibaba, eBay, and Mercado Libre are based on offering consumers products from thousands of different providers rather than their own stock. "Gig economy" apps offer services such as rides, lodging, or delivery of food or groceries from firms and individuals. Other services rely on content such as video (YouTube, Vimeo), opinions and reviews of products or services (Yelp, Google), or information (blogs) developed by thousands of users, most of whom remain relatively unknown to the final consumer. The relationship between these intermediaries (websites and apps) and the firms or individuals offering their own products or services is therefore essential to the functioning of those digital transactions. These connections are also possible to the extent that businesses and consumers around the world can transmit the relevant information (i.e., data) in a timely and efficient manner.

### i. Cross-border data

**Cross-border data flows are an increasingly essential element of international trade.** Data flows not only support trade in goods, making production and distribution more effective and less costly, but such flows are in fact the vehicle for trading digital services across borders. As trade in global digital services has increased dramatically in recent years, so have global data flows. Regulation of data flows, especially of personal data, lies at the heart of digital trade governance. More than 40 percent of US firms surveyed by the US International Trade Commission (USITC) consider data localization requirements and market access regulations to be obstacles to trade, particularly larger firms and those in the digital communications, content, and retail services sectors (USITC, 2014). Assessing regulatory restrictions on digital trade, Ferracane and Van Der Marel (2021b) find that restrictive data policies, specifically those related to the cross-border movement of data, result in lower imports of data-intense services for the countries imposing them, which translates into lower levels of cross-border digital trade.

**Restrictions on global data flows can also burden domestic companies using digital technologies,**


particularly in the context of global value chains (World Bank, 2020). Swedish manufacturing firms recently reported that data localization requirements and restrictions on cross-border data flows, including for outward transfers, adversely affect the setup and operation of their global production networks (NBT, 2015). The challenge for policy makers is promoting the sharing and transfer of data in a manner that supports the economic benefits of digital trade, while ensuring that sensitive information remains secure and the relevant regulations on personal data protection are respected.

**Restrictions on data flows, commonly referred as "data localization" requirements, take multiple forms.** Limitations on the management of data are not limited to the explicit regime on cross-border transfers: provisions mandating that certain type of data be stored, processed, or accessed only with the jurisdiction of the country—often referred as "data localization" measures—are also critical in the management of information. Data storage requirements entail the obligations to house certain data on servers physically located in the country. Local processing requirements, on the other hand, mandate that all handling of such data—such as updating, anonymizing, or preparing for any specific use—must take place within the country. Local access requirements require that certain information can only be retrieved from within the country. While these rules do not prohibit cross-border data sharing or offshore storage of copies, they impose significant costs by physically anchoring the data to domestic infrastructure.

**Broad, economy-wide, data localization requirements are rare, but are not unusual in specific sectors.** No country in the DTRR features general data localizations for personal and non-personal data, but 12 countries in the database have adopted data localization requirements for all personal data—a requirement often considered a major restriction to cross-border data flows. Ten countries from that group demand local storage of all personal data, while two more mandate domestic processing instead, including Kenya which features both.

## Non-personal data

**Non-personal data flows are important for digital trade, enhancing commercial benefits and technological innovation.** Non-personal data plays a key role in digital trade by enabling commercial benefits, driving technological innovation, and facilitating global data flows essential for international business operations. Unlike personal data, it is usually not subject to the same amount of privacy restrictions—such as consent principles or data minimization principles. This relative flexibility in handling and cross-border sharing makes non-personal data especially valuable for improving trade efficiency, enhancing public services, and supporting competitive market strategies. Understanding and managing the flow of non-personal data is key to maximizing the benefits of digital trade while ensuring secure and efficient global trade exchanges.

### ii. Intermediary liability

**Rules on intermediary liability can make or break business models based on digital platforms.** Intermediary liability rules are the set of provisions that distribute the liability between intermediaries (website and apps) and actual vendors or content developers when things go wrong. In other words, intermediary liability is the responsibility that falls upon online intermediaries, such as search engines, application platforms, social networks, and broadband companies, for third-party content featured in, or products and services offered through, their websites or apps. Just as intermediation is not a novel business model, intermediary liability rules are not a new legal concept—most such rules can be traced back to Roman law. Intermediary liability rules can, in fact, be broader rules that apply to online intermediaries (Gasser and Schulz 2015). However, specific rules of digital intermediaries are more likely to adapt to the specific conditions of digital markets. Lack of provisions specifically oriented to digital platforms leads to courts' decisions to fill the gaps with rules and principles not tailored to the digital context—often with odd or suboptimal results. Even where judicial decisions reach optimal outcomes, having explicit regulations in place increases transparency and predictability, thereby reducing costs for digital business and strengthening user rights.

**Given the central role of digital platforms, clear rules on intermediary liability are essential to digital trade.** Social media and e-commerce platforms are hallmarks of digital markets, whose inherently global nature provides a natural environment for connecting suppliers and consumers around worldwide. The network effects of digital platforms tend to concentrate market power in a few massive global providers. However, the growth of these services can be hindered by outdated or inadequate rules on intermediary liability, which may impose harsh penalties for user-generated content or behavior. This regulatory uncertainty also affects domestic start-ups, which may face prohibitive costs if held responsible for their users' misconduct.

**Regulations on intermediary liability typically touch on two different policy areas: intellectual property and criminal law.** Legal liability for digital platforms may arise from a user's vendor infringement of intellectual property rights. This is the case, for instance, when a vendor offers a counterfeit product that is in violation of trademarks, or uploads a video, song or text in violation of the author's copyright. More egregious conduct may amount to violations of criminal law, for example, if a user uploads a video calling for violence, publishes a deep-fake nude image of a celebrity, or issues a threat to or otherwise harms another person. A comprehensive framework on intermediary liability would cover both types of rules.

## DTRR DATABASE METHODOLOGY

## A. Data collection and review methodology

**The DTRR data is based on the direct review of laws and regulations.** The DTRR reflects the regulatory solutions as enshrined in laws, decrees, and other major legal instruments. The strict application of the traceability principle implies that, with the two exceptions identified below, the data featured in the DTRR originates solely from the actual legal framework of the country in question. In a handful of cases, third-party sources reported on the existence of certain regulatory measures, yet no legal instrument could be found to review, confirm, and trace back to that regulation; in those cases, no measure was recorded. Once collected, the legal instruments were carefully reviewed by a team of WB staff and consultants with legal expertise and background. Overall, the DTRR incorporated information from approximately 700 legal instruments and features over 5,200 references to specific provisions.

**The data are current as of March 2024 and do not reflect any changes to the legislation after that date.** Despite intensive efforts, there were some countries where information could not be gathered. On a few occasions, media or other sources suggested that legislation did exist, but it could not be found online. Because of these potential gaps, the database records missing regulations as "no provision was identified" under the relevant regulatory solution, rather than "no provision exists." Should interested readers find gaps in the information, they are welcome to contact the authors.

### i. Traceability to its original source

**DTRR's information is supported by direct reference to the legal instruments where they are featured.** With a view to ensuring the quality of the information, each datapoint can be traced back to the specific provision, paragraph, and/or section number that provides for that specific regulatory solution in the country's legal framework. For example, the data point indicating that *Argentina's framework provides that electronic signatures issued abroad have the same legal effect as domestic electronic signature* stems from Article 16 of Argentina's Digital Signature Law of 2001. Traceability information is readily available to interested parties through the download of the main database file.

**"Traceability" ensures the accuracy of the information, and eases any corrections needed.** Identifying the specific legal source of the information not only ensures the accuracy of the information but also allows for it to be quickly amended it in case of reforms or new developments in the underlying regulatory framework. This feature is oriented to facilitating informed policy-making and identifying specific needs to improve or reform existing regulatory framework.

**This feature sets the DTRR apart from survey-based and from web-scraping database.** While some databases attempt to complement indicator-based information with qualitative information about their sources, the main survey-based databases in the field focus on offering a quantitative index that measures the regulatory framework's quality and offer little or no access information on the sources of those values. Databases focused on capturing information online ("web-scraping," whether in an automated or manual way) rely instead on third party sources, such as news media, other databases, and online reports, rather than actual interpretation of the legal instruments. This has the advantage of capturing a much broader range of information, including on how those regulatory measures are applied in practice, but often misses the link to the underlying legal instruments and forces the users to trust the cited third-party source without the ability to verify it directly.

### ii. Scope of the data collection

**Some procedural steps constrained the scope of data collection for the DTRR.** Like the proverbial needle, finding one specific measure that exists in the whole regulatory haystack of a given country can be a time-consuming exercise. To prevent an infinite search, the team adopted a standardized procedure that delivered substantial results while keeping the data collection effort at a feasible scale. The first step of data collection entailed reviewing a number of online databases, repositories, books, studies and similar services that offer legislative information on areas covered by the DTRR. Broad databases like UNCTAD Cyberlaw Tracker, OneTrust DataGuidance, ECIPE's Digital Trade Restrictiveness database and the Digital Trade Integration database were particularly helpful in finding and accessing regulations. # iii. Interpretation

**The regulatory reviews were guided by a few principles focused on the intent behind the rules.** While the database builds on the notion that certain key measures are at the basis of digital trade regulation worldwide, how those laws are drafted and incorporated into the regulatory framework can vary greatly. A same provision may be found featured as a mandate ("shall" ["Trust services provided by trust service providers established in a third country *shall be recognised* as legally equivalent to qualified trust services provided by qualified trust service providers established in the Union where …"] ) or as a prohibition ("A data message, electronic document, electronic record or other communication *must not* be denied legal protection, effect, validity, or enforceability on the ground that it is in electronic form" ). Even a prohibition can be expressed through positive language ("It *shall be unlawful* for any person to use a bot to communicate or interact with another person in California online, with the intent to mislead the other person about its artificial identity…" ), or negative language ("Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs … *shall be prohibited*." ), or even neutral language but associated to a sanction. Reviewing thousands of regulatory measures from around the world in order to decide whether or not they meet one specific definition necessarily entails a cautious interpretative effort. Even a meticulous reading, however, cannot eliminate occasional doubts and some level of judgement value. Further, to limit the impact of such uncertainties and to make sure that all regulations were being assessed through the same lens, some teleological principles were defined to help the reviewers in the interpretation, namely:

1. **Context:** when the language of the provision(s) was not clear enough to determine the answer to our question, context was taken into consideration. This includes the goal of the legislation, whether the legislation was following a given instrument (model law, other country's regulation, etc.). In addition, the position of the provision was considered, for example, what provisions are adjacent, what title and section the provision is housed under, among others.

2. **Governing Ministry/Agency:** the authority over the law will commonly have layman's explanations on their website via Q&As, blog posts, news reports, or short explanatory guides.

3. **Legal blogs and regulatory research websites:** big law firms and domestic specialty firms generally post short blogs regarding recent legislation as well as articles analyzing or describing legal developments. In addition, regulatory research websites (DTI, WIPO, Library of Congress) may have final conclusory data on regulations or links to explanatory texts.

4. **Pro-measure:** when, despite all other interpretative efforts, a provision (or combination thereof) left doubts about whether it in fact featured a specific measure, it was recorded as the regulation being in place—the most favorable interpretation for the country. The opposite is true for the handful of "negative" measures included in the database, particularly those related to cross-border data flows.

# iv. Deviations I: Restrictions on Cross-border Data Flows

**International flows of machine-readable data are essential for digital trade, especially for trade in services** (WDR2021). Specifically, cross-border trade in services relies on information traveling through telecommunications networks (i.e., digital data) to deliver the services from suppliers to consumers located around the world. Digital data plays in digital services much like shipping containers do in merchandise trade: it serves as the medium for the content of the transaction to reach its consumer. Regulatory restrictions on the cross-border movement of data are therefore the effective equivalent to direct restrictions on international trade on digital services.

**Deviating from its own philosophy, the DTRR includes a set of restrictive regulatory solutions to cross-border data flows.** Given the critical role that data flows play in digital trade, it was decided that regulatory restrictions on cross-border data flows, in particular limitations to cross-border transfers and data localization requirements, was inherent to a database focused on digital trade regulation. The DTRR section on cross-border regulations therefore deviates from other DTRR sections as it features both "positive" regulatory solutions that contribute to an enabling environment to digital trade, as well as "negative" regulatory solutions that entail limitations to cross-border data flows, thus hampering the ability to engage in services trade. Nineteen of the 34 regulatory measures collected under the section capture different types of limitations to cross-border data flows.

**Regulatory restrictions on cross-border data are sandboxed for the purposes of analysis and visualizations of the regulatory environment.** When assessing the progress and "readiness" of the regulatory framework for digital trade, restrictions on cross-border data were considered separately from all other data points in the data. Since the DTRR builds on the notion that a comprehensive and modern regulatory framework acts as an enabler for digital trade, broad analyses and descriptions based on the DTRR, especially aggregate comparative figures that describe the "readiness" to digital trade, effectively exclude all 19 datapoints on data restrictions from the visualizations and analysis. However, these data are brought to light when looking specifically at regulations on cross-border data flows, and wherever reference to data limitations is warranted.

# C. Caveats

# When using the DTRR database, it is important to be aware of its limitations.

Reviewing laws and regulations from across the globe required certain compromises to make the data suitable for tabulation and user-friendly comparison. These compromises affect how the information is collected, reviewed, and presented, and should be considered when interpreting the database.

### i. Sub-set of regulation

# The exercise did not review the entirety of laws and regulations enacted by all covered countries.

Collecting and reading every legal instrument in the world would make for a very accurate, but unfortunately not a cost-efficient database. The data collection was thus based on a specific procedure and sub-set of regulations likely to deliver comprehensive and reliable results within a reasonable effort and budget—as explained in Section 3. This means that there may be some regulatory measures in place that have not been recorded in the database. In fact, in a few cases (less than a handful) there were third-party reports indicating that some measures were in place, but no legal instruments could be found to support that information. Users of the DTRR be aware that different methodological decisions carry certain implications.

# Regulatory solutions may exist beyond those featured in the DTRR.

This may be the case especially for low-income countries who have not digitalized their legal information. Further, some regulatory areas, like paperless trade, consumer protection, and questions on digital ID, are often governed by decrees or ministerial directive or guidelines that are less likely to be made available online. However, given the breadth of the review, it is possible that specific regulatory measures were missed, but unlikely that instruments governing entire regulatory areas were omitted.

### ii. Legal interpretation

# Interpreting regulations, especially from foreign legal instruments, is not an exact science.

The interpretations of regulations, especially when read directly from legal instruments, invariably involves a degree of subjectivity. As a result, it is possible that the reviewers misread, and thereby misclassified the information. The reviewing team sought to mitigate this risk by ensuring that their reading was in line with other literature as well as the classification in other databases. In particular, the team collaborated closely with the authors of the Digital Trade Integration Project^(11)—the online database most closely related to DTRR—to ensure that interpretations were aligned. However, some regulatory measures may be interpreted differently across databases, or in comparison to how they are intended in the source country.

### iii. Implementation / de facto Regulation

# The dataset does not account for the implementation or enforcement of existing legislation.

The information reflected in the database focuses on *de jure* regulatory solutions as captured in mandatory legal instruments. The data does not capture whether or how those measures are being implemented, or whether other informal *de facto* measures apply. This limits the understanding of the effective regulatory framework applying to digital trade, especially in middle- and low-income countries with weak governance practices, where the gaps between written rules and actual enforcement and monitoring practices by government agencies is often substantial. Some studies have focused on assessing implementation and enforcement of regulations on specific policy areas, such as on consumer protection (OECD, 2018), based on surveys or questionnaires answered by policymakers or practitioners. On the contrary, this "weakness" of the DTRR of capturing only de jure information is the result of its methodological approach to strictly feature data directly referenced to formal regulatory instruments, ensuring "traceability" of the information.

# iii. Different legal systems

# It is important to note that the DTRR does not account for differences in legal systems or the various sources of law they may recognize.

Instead, it focuses exclusively on written laws and regulations adopted by legislative or executive bodies. Legal systems around the world differ in the sources they recognize for establishing general rules. One key distinction is between civil law and common law traditions. Civil law systems rely primarily on written instruments—laws and regulations enacted by legislatures or, in some cases, adopted by the executive branch under specific conditions. In contrast, common law systems, while also recognizing written legislation, give legal weight to judicial decisions. These decisions,

Another layer of variation arises from how power is distributed within a country. In federal systems, legal authority is shared across multiple levels of government—such as federal, state, or provincial—whereas unitary systems centralize power at the national level. Additionally, some jurisdictions incorporate customary law, which derives from long-standing local traditions or the authority of local or religious leaders. Given the diversity of legal traditions globally, the DTRR standardizes its approach by focusing exclusively on written laws and regulations—typically those adopted by the legislative or executive branches—rather than attempting to account for all possible sources of legal authority.

**In practice, few differences exist when it comes to technical economic regulations.** The review of regulations on digital trade did not yield major differences between countries featuring these different legal systems. Economic regulations, specifically those focused on digital transactions, are only marginally affected by customary or religious rules, which tend to govern more traditional legislative aspects, like the criminal and civil justice—in fact, countries based on a customary or religious system also tend to adopt written rules on more modern and complex aspects of legislation, including digital trade. Indeed, the review did not find significant differences between common-law and civil-law systems. Granular matters related to market regulations are rarely regulated through judicial decisions, especially in technical issues like electronic transactions, cybersecurity, or data protection. In fact, comprehensive regulations were found in all regulatory areas in the common law countries under review. However, in common law systems, certain regulatory solutions may not be explicitly written into legislation if they are already established through judicial decisions. As a result, the DTRR may undercount some areas of regulation in these systems. This is particularly relevant for consumer protection and intermediary liability, which is often shaped by long-standing case law on commercial matters, even outside of digital contexts. Still, the decision to adopt comprehensive online consumer protection regulation appears to reflect policy choices more than legal tradition, as some common law countries—such as the United Kingdom—have introduced extensive statutory frameworks in this area.

When gathering laws, the issue of federalist countries commonly gets brought up. This is because most countries in the world subscribe to a unitary form of government, which means that there is one central legal framework that rules over the country. Gathering laws on unitary forms of government is, therefore, much simpler than the alternative—countries that subscribe to federalism. Federalism breaks apart the single system of government into a tiered system, which is usually a state or provincial level and a federal level. In a federal system, both state and national governments hold legal authority. Because states can pass their own laws, a single country may have legal differences across jurisdictions. However, state laws cannot conflict federal laws, which take precedence.

To ensure that a uniform application of the laws was applied when gathering data on the countries, the review did not include data below the federal level. Including laws from state or provincial levels could result in conflicting rules across jurisdictions or inconsistent legal coverage, as not all subnational entities adopt similar legislation. Thus, only federal legal rules were counted: national laws, national regulations, executive/legislative/federal agency decrees, and binding federal agency rules.

However, a case study on the United States was performed to better understand the impact of federalism in a large digital economy. Three states were studied based on the size of their large economies: California, New York, and Texas. All three states include data from both federal and state-level laws, offering a more robust picture of how federal systems function in practice. The findings only significantly differed over new legislation, such as data protection, AI, and cybersecurity. Logically, these findings match with predictions likely because state governments tend to move faster than federal governments that have more competing interests.

## v. Presenting regulatory "Readiness" trends: quintiles

**Regulatory "readiness" to digital trade hinges on a comprehensive and modern legal framework, more than specific regulatory solutions.** An enabling legal environment for digital trade depends on providing effective measures to tackle the novel challenges of digital, international markets. These challenges are addressed by specific regulatory solutions like the ones captured in the DTRR. However, few regulatory measures are by themselves determinants of an enabling environment for digital trade; instead overcoming the challenges of digital markets typically require a set of various interrelated regulatory measures contributing to the overarching policy goal. For example, multiple regulatory measures can act as safeguards for personal privacy, including explicit limitations on the legal basis for data collection and processing, ensuring a right to access such information by data subjects, the "right for be forgotten," and specialized and stricter rules on biometric data; while all these measures contribute to the same, the absence of one or some of them does not necessarily make it a poor framework. As a result, when assessing the "readiness" of given regulatory area it is important to consider the regulation as a whole rather than the presence or absence of a specific safeguard.[^12]

**Graphs based on categories, rather than specific values, help visualize this approach.** To reflect the importance of looking at regulations broadly, rather than merely counting regulatory solutions, it is advisable that analyses based on the DTRR data be best featured in stepped categories of regulatory progress. The DTRR website, for example, portrays regulatory readiness in five categories (quintiles) depending on the percentage of positive answers to the regulatory solutions considered in each regulatory area.

**The DTRR database is a dynamic platform that provides stakeholders from various sectors with critical insights and data to shape and enhance digital trade policies globally—supporting legal, economic, and policy development.** The DTRR database is designed to be an instrumental resource for a diverse array of stakeholders, including developing countries, academic researchers, digital trade companies, and others engaged in the realm of digital trade.

It can guide the formulation and refinement of their own digital trade policies, fostering an environment conducive to digital economic growth and integration into the global digital economy.

### iv. Analytical and Quantitative work

**The database offers comprehensive regulatory data and analytical resources, assisting stakeholders in effectively developing and implementing informed digital trade policies.** The DTRR database serves as both a comprehensive repository of global digital trade regulations and a robust analytical tool that transforms raw data into actionable insights. This dual functionality makes the DTRR database an indispensable resource for policymakers, industry experts, and researchers focused on the intricacies of digital trade. In practical terms, the database supports detailed analysis of digital trade practices by providing users with access to extensive regulatory information from various countries and sectors. This enables users to conduct comparative analyses, identify regulatory trends, and benchmark against global standards. Companies engaged in cross-border e-commerce, for example, can analyze entry requirements, consumer protection laws, and data privacy regulations across multiple jurisdictions to effectively strategize their market expansion plans.

Based on the previous work done for WDR2021, a deeper analysis of cross-border data transfer regimes can be effectively conducted using the DTRR database. The comprehensive insights and foundational frameworks established in WDR2021 provide a solid groundwork for further exploration. By leveraging the extensive data and detailed regulatory information within the DTRR database, researchers and policymakers can gain a nuanced understanding of how different regulatory regimes impact the flow of non-personal data across borders. This analysis is crucial for identifying best practices, potential barriers, and opportunities to enhance global digital trade and data governance.

**Additionally, the DTRR database enables users to model and predict the effects of regulatory changes on their economies or businesses.** This functionality supports scenario analysis, risk assessment, and economic impact studies, which are crucial for making informed decisions. By quantifying the potential effects of new regulations, users can develop strong business strategies and policy frameworks that proactively address market developments.

**Access to comprehensive regulatory data allows policymakers to compare their national frameworks with those of other countries, facilitating insights into areas of strength and potential improvement.** This capability supports evidence-based policymaking and aids in the development of policies that promote a competitive and efficient digital trade environment. It also enables policymakers to align their regulations with global best practices and international standards, ensuring that their legal frameworks support digital trade while safeguarding consumer rights and promoting data security.

### v. Feedback

**Recognizing the importance of user input in the continuous development and refinement of the DTRR database, a comprehensive feedback system will be implemented.** This system will include regular user surveys and an open channel for ongoing comments and suggestions. Feedback collected through these mechanisms will be systematically reviewed and used to guide updates and enhancements to the database, ensuring it remains relevant and useful. The goal is to establish a development process centered around users, involving them directly in the evolution of the database. This strategy not only enhances the accuracy and relevance of the database but also builds a sense of community and ownership among users, ultimately improving their experience and satisfaction.

## LOOKING AHEAD

One day, people will wonder how global trade was even possible before goods and services were seamlessly bought and sold in global digital markets without regard to or even knowledge of where vendors were located. We are not there yet. For the time being, digital trade offers a mixed picture across countries and sectors. In some countries it is thriving, and in others it is still a new, unusual experience. While some areas of regulation evolve quickly in response to new technologies, others lag behind or struggle to adjust to the pace and complexity of digital change. And, for the most part, digital trade is smooth and effective until something goes wrong, and then it becomes chaotic and hopeless as entailing strong reputational damages.
