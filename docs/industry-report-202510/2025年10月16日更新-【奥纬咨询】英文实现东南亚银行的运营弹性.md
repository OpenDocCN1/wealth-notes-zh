## OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE

How banks can prepare, adapt, and thrive amid rising risks

### FIVE PRIORITIES FOR BANKS IN SOUTHEAST ASIA TO BUILD OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE

Jonas Heckmann

Julian Granger-Bevan

Maksim Ryabukhin

Thomas Garside

---

Operational resilience is the capability of a bank to continue delivering its critical operations and services during periods of disruption, according to The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision.

Achieving this has become both increasingly challenging and vital. Banks today face a volatile environment marked by escalating cyber threats, technology interdependencies, complex third-party relationships, and potential global disruptions, from pandemics to large-scale technology incidents.

In this context, resilience cannot be treated merely as a compliance obligation. It must be a strategic priority for boards and C-suite executives seeking to protect customers, safeguard reputation, maintain competitiveness, ensure long-term viability, and uphold financial system stability.

Since the Basel Committee published its Principles for Operational Resilience in 2021, regulators worldwide have introduced frameworks with the shared goal of protecting customers and preserving financial stability.

Drawing on global experience, Oliver Wyman has supported more than 15 banks across North America, Europe (including preparations for the Digital Operational Resilience Act [DORA]), and Australia. We expect Southeast Asian banks to follow a similar path as regional regulators formalize their own frameworks, integrating business continuity, IT risk, and third-party risk management into a comprehensive, harmonized approach.

This report draws on extensive conversations with regulators, boards, and C-suite executives across Southeast Asia. It distils their most pressing concerns, challenges, and insights into five critical priorities to help banks meet regulatory expectations, embed resilience at the heart of operations, and strengthen their ability to protect customers and financial stability.

---

The following sections explore five critical priorities for Southeast Asian banks as they strengthen their operational resilience:

- Clarify what operational resilience means — and how it complements, rather than replaces, business continuity management.
- Define critical business services and set impact tolerances to focus resources where customer and market impact is greatest.
- Manage third- and fourth-party risks amid growing reliance on cloud providers and interconnected ecosystems.
- Navigate the operational resilience journey by learning from global leaders and aligning strategies with regulatory expectations.
- Establish clear ownership and governance to embed accountability across the organization.

### PRIORITY 1

**CLARIFY WHAT OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE MEANS — AND HOW IT COMPLEMENTS, RATHER THAN REPLACES, BUSINESS CONTINUITY MANAGEMENT**

Operational resilience is a capability within a bank's non-financial risk management framework. It is not neatly tied to any particular risk event;¹ instead, it represents the capability to deliver critical business services — a narrowly defined set of essential services — through severe but plausible disruptions. These are typically events lasting several days, such as a third-party provider failure, a natural disaster, or a prolonged system outage.

Crucially, operational resilience does not replace business continuity management but rather complements it. However, the boundaries between the two often require clarification, especially because many regulatory frameworks integrate both topics into a single set of requirements. Examples include:

- The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority's (APRA) CPS 230, which combines resilience and business continuity management in its Prudential Standard on Operational Risk Management
- Monetary Authority of Singapore's (MAS) Guidelines on Business Continuity Management, which already incorporate operational resilience concepts such as critical business services and customer impact
- Bank Negara Malaysia's (BNM) Policy Document on Business Continuity Management, which also adopts many resilience principles within its broader approach

¹ Risk event, as defined by the ORX ("a discrete, specific occurrence, one degree removed from the impact on the organization or its stakeholders"); orx.org.

---

While these guidelines often bring the two topics together, operational resilience and business continuity management serve distinct objectives, scopes, and success measures:

**Exhibit 1: Comparison of business continuity management and operational resilience**

| | **Business continuity management** | **Operational resilience** |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Recovering and restoring **internal operations and systems** to a pre-disruption state<br><br>Assumes that recovery objectives are achievable | Assumes disruptions are inevitable and focuses on critical operations to **meet customer and market expectations through the disruption**<br><br>Assumes that recovery objectives cannot be met |
| **Scope** | **Narrow**: Internal functions, processes, and IT systems | **Broad**: Covers people, processes, technology, facilities, and third-party dependencies, including interconnectedness of the financial system |
| **Key metrics/objectives** | Recovery time objectives (RTOs) and recovery point objectives (RPOs) | Impact tolerances in terms of customer and market impact |
| **Scenarios** | Focuses on **testing recovery plans** for specific systems or functions to ensure restoration within RTOs/RPOs<br><br>Example scenarios:<br>- Key system is down for 4 hours<br>- Power outage at a data center | Looks at "severe but plausible" scenarios and systemic risks, to validate the bank's ability to remain within **impact tolerances**<br><br>Example scenarios:<br>- Cyberattack results in corruption of all outgoing data<br>- Deletion of customer database, taking 5+ days to rebuild |
| **Governance** | Managed by dedicated business continuity management teams, often within risk management or IT departments | Driven by senior leadership and the board, with a strong emphasis on enterprise-wide collaboration and accountability |
| **Time horizon** | Typically a few hours | Typically 3 to 5 days |
| **Scale** | **300-700 plans** | **15-20 critical operations** |

### PRIORITY 2

**DEFINE CRITICAL BUSINESS SERVICES AND SET IMPACT TOLERANCES**

**DEFINING CRITICAL BUSINESS SERVICES AT THE FIRST LEVEL**

A bank's operational resilience journey begins with defining and aligning on critical business services — the essential services delivered to customers and the wider financial system. Every subsequent decision about resilience, from investments to impact tolerances, flows from this definition.

The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) defines critical business services as those that, if disrupted beyond agreed tolerance levels, would cause material harm to customers or the financial system. While regulators leave institutions to determine which services qualify, they offer guidance through "expected lists." For example, BNM highlights services such as ATM and cash deposit access, online and mobile banking, payment card services, call center support, and payment authorization, clearing, and settlement.

Not all important services qualify as "critical" under operational resilience. Distinguishing between the two helps prioritize efforts effectively. Common examples include:

- **Know Your Customer (KYC) onboarding**: Key for compliance but short-term disruptions rarely affect customers broadly.
- **Internal payroll**: Vital for employee morale but delays of several days rarely threaten stability.
- **Payments system**: Foundational technology, but resilience focuses on how critical services rely on it, not on the system's resilience itself, which falls under business continuity management frameworks.

**REFINING CRITICAL BUSINESS SERVICES AT THE SECOND LEVEL**

After drafting an initial list, banks often refine definitions to focus on where customer impact occurs. Without this step, services risk being defined too broadly, ambitions become unrealistic, and resilience investments spread too thin.

For example, "settlement of payment transactions" sounds straightforward, but requires precision. Does it include all or only large value payments? Domestic and international? All channels or only selected ones? These clarifications are essential for credible, achievable impact tolerances.

## DEFINING IMPACT TOLERANCES

Once critical business services are defined, banks must set impact tolerances — thresholds for how much disruption can occur before causing material harm to customers. How these tolerances are then achieved depends on the capabilities of the bank.

Two examples illustrate this practice. Monzo², a UK-based digital bank, built a fully independent stand-in platform on a separate provider, ensuring uninterrupted 24/7 payments.

By contrast, a US brick-and-mortar bank³ set a flexible tolerance. In a severe but plausible scenario, it processes 85% of payments above $500,000 and 85% of smaller priority payments received before 4pm by day's end, using manual backups and reconciliation procedures that supplement automated workflows.

### PRIORITY 3

## MANAGE THIRD- AND FOURTH-PARTY RISKS AMID GROWING RELIANCE ON CLOUD PROVIDERS

Banks now operate within highly interconnected ecosystems, relying on a complex network of external vendors, suppliers, and service providers to deliver critical operations. These dependencies are especially pronounced with cloud providers and other technology platforms that underpin digital banking services.

Operational resilience takes a holistic view of this landscape: banks must be able to deliver critical business services regardless of the type of disruption and whether delivery relies on third or even fourth parties. Achieving this requires a structured approach to managing external dependencies, ensuring that resilience extends well beyond the bank's own systems and processes.

Through our work with leading institutions, we have observed four strategies banks are using to manage these dependencies effectively:

- **1. Strengthen due diligence and contracting**

When a critical business service depends on a third-party provider, that provider must itself be resilient. For smaller vendors, banks often conduct their own assessments, while for large cloud providers — such as AWS, Microsoft, or Google — they may rely on the provider's attestations and published certifications. Due diligence goes hand in hand with contractual alignment: where necessary, banks require updates to existing agreements to ensure resilience expectations are explicitly built into vendor obligations.

- **2. Minimize concentration risks**

Overreliance on a single third party can create significant vulnerability. Banks must avoid situations where one provider is responsible for both primary delivery of a critical business service and the associated stand-in or backup processes. Diversification is critical to maintaining continuity in the event of disruption.

- **3. Understand fourth-party risk**

Risks do not stop with third parties. Banks are increasingly exposed to fourth-party providers — the suppliers and platforms their vendors depend on. For example, multiple providers may rely on the same underlying technology platform, creating hidden concentration risks. To manage this, banks are increasingly requiring disclosure of key fourth-party relationships and incorporating these dependencies into their resilience planning.

- **4. Involve third parties in scenario testing**

Testing severe but plausible scenarios is central to demonstrating operational resilience. Banks are increasingly inviting third parties to participate in these exercises to validate their ability to deliver critical business services under stress and to identify weaknesses across the end-to-end delivery chain.

### PRIORITY 4

## NAVIGATE THE OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE JOURNEY

A typical operational resilience journey takes two to three years to transition into a business-as-usual capability. Banks in Southeast Asia, however, start with several advantages. Many core concepts are already embedded in existing regulations, such as business continuity management, IT risk management, and outsourcing frameworks. Institutions can also learn from jurisdictions where operational resilience standards are more mature, including North America, Europe, and Australia. There is also strong awareness and support from boards and the C-suite.

Most banks Oliver Wyman has supported on their operational resilience journey have followed a structured five-step process, as illustrated below:

**Exhibit 2: Key steps in building and running a sustainable operational resilience program**

| Step | Title | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **1** | **Foundation setting and program planning** | Validate existing foundations and develop resilience framework |
| **2** | **Prioritization of critical services** | Define a set list of critical services to prioritize for operational resilience improvement |
| **3** | **End-to-end mapping** | Map critical business services end-to-end at the appropriate level of granularity |
| **4** | **Defining an impact tolerance** | Define and calibrate the impact tolerance for a 'severe but plausible' scenario, given the firm's risk appetite |
| **5** | **Scenario testing** | Conduct a 'severe but plausible' shock event to assess the business' ability to meet the impact tolerance |

Source: Oliver Wyman analysis

Building an effective operational resilience program, however, is not easy. It demands new skillsets, closer integration of risk, IT, and business functions, and a cultural shift from "operational resilience is IT's responsibility" to "operational resilience is everyone's responsibility." It also requires fundamental changes to how the organization operates.

From our work with banks, five key lessons stand out:

- **1. Deliver business value.** Treating operational resilience purely as a compliance exercise is a missed opportunity. Programs should achieve business objectives as well as risk management ambitions.

- **2. Build strong foundations.** Establish a clear framework and align on critical business services. Institutions that rush these steps often face costly setbacks.

- **3. Start with a pilot.** Focus first on one or two critical business services to test the approach, adjust early, and build the organizational muscles needed to scale.

- **4. Integrate operational resilience into the technology roadmap.** Embedding it into architecture design, system upgrades, and vendor selection ensures sustainability without requiring immediate transformation.

- **5. Make resilience part of the culture.** When viewed as a strategic enabler rather than a compliance "tick-the-box" exercise, operational resilience strengthens market position, protects reputation, and safeguards long-term viability.

### PRIORITY 5

## ESTABLISH CLEAR OWNERSHIP AND GOVERNANCE

Operational resilience is a bank-wide responsibility, typically initiated and overseen by the group board. Boards play an active role in shaping the bank's resilience ambitions — challenging priorities, approving plans, and making key decisions such as defining critical business services and setting impact tolerance levels.

While the group board sets direction, the group risk function usually drives the early stages of the journey. This includes establishing frameworks and policies, integrating them into the broader non-financial risk management strategy, facilitating the identification of critical business services and impact tolerances, and running and scaling the program, including scenario testing.

However, accountability always rests with the business. Ownership of each critical business service — and its corresponding impact tolerance — should sit with a senior executive or executive minus one, even if parts of the service are delivered by other divisions. This ensures clear, end-to-end accountability for resilience outcomes.

In banks with a mature Three Lines of Defense framework, some institutions also appoint a Chief Resilience Officer, often within group operations. This role typically coordinates operational resilience activities across the enterprise, while group risk focuses on advisory and assurance responsibilities.




### Five Priorities For Banks In Southeast Asia To Build Operational Resilience

**CONCLUSION**

The urgency for Southeast Asian banks to accelerate their operational resilience agenda has never been greater. While many institutions are laying the groundwork by developing policies and frameworks, the rapidly evolving threat landscape and heightened regulatory scrutiny demand swift and decisive action.

Some leading banks have already made progress by identifying their critical business services, but these efforts must now be broadened and deepened across the region to mitigate escalating operational risks. Regulators are moving quickly to formalize resilience requirements and intensify supervisory oversight, particularly for domestically or regionally systemically important financial institutions. Failure to act decisively risks not only regulatory penalties but also severe operational and reputational damage, undermining both stability and customer trust. The time to embed resilience at the core of banking operations is now — delays will only increase exposure to disruptive events and threaten the future viability of institutions in this dynamic, high-stakes environment.

Meeting this challenge requires a collective commitment from all stakeholders — not only to comply with regulatory expectations but to position operational resilience as a core component of strategic success. Embracing this journey will safeguard institutional stability, bolster customer confidence, and ensure long-term sustainability in an increasingly volatile environment.
